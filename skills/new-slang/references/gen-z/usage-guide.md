# Usage guide — how to not get clocked

Vocabulary is the easy 20%. Everything that actually outs someone as a tourist is in here: dosage, syntax, typography, and knowing which register you're standing in.

---

## 1. Dosage — the number one mistake

Beginners treat slang like seasoning to be poured on. Fluent speakers use it like a **precision instrument: one word, doing real work, in an otherwise ordinary sentence.**

> ❌ "No cap fam, that deck absolutely slaps, it's giving main character energy fr fr, you ate and left no crumbs bestie 💯🔥"
>
> ✅ "that deck ate"

The second is fluent. The first is a hostage video.

**The rule: roughly one slang move per 1–2 sentences, maximum.** The frame of the sentence stays plain English. The slang goes in the **predicate** — the part doing the describing — not scattered through the scaffolding.

> ✅ "I read the whole proposal last night and honestly it's giving rushed."
>
> The first clause is completely normal English. All the work happens in three words at the end. That contrast is what makes it land.

**Corollary:** if you remove the slang word and the sentence still says the same thing, the slang was decorative — cut it. Slang should be the most efficient word available, not a costume.

## 2. The syntax patterns — where fluency actually lives

Words go stale in months. **These constructions have lasted years and generate infinite new sentences.** Learning ten patterns beats learning a hundred words. This is the highest-leverage section in the skill.

### `it's giving ___`
Assigns a vibe by naming something else it resembles. The slot takes a noun, an adjective, or a whole scenario. Enormously productive.
- "it's giving unpaid intern"
- "the new landing page is giving 2013 startup"
- "it's giving desperate"

### `not me ___ing`
Self-deprecating confession of something mildly embarrassing you just did.
- "not me pushing to main on a Friday"
- "not me rereading that text 40 times"

### `the way ___`
Incredulous emphasis. Points at something absurd. Note: the sentence often has **no main clause** — that's correct, don't finish it.
- "the way this repo has zero tests"
- "the way he just said that out loud in the meeting"

### `___ era`
Frames a phase of life as a narrative chapter. Fond, ironic, self-aware.
- "in my refactoring era"
- "she's in her villain era"

### `I fear ___`
Softens bad news with mock-formality. Genuinely useful at work — it's polite *and* current.
- "I fear the migration didn't run"
- "I fear we've been optimizing the wrong metric"

### `no because ___`
**Does not mean "no."** It's a sincere escalation — "wait, seriously, listen." One of the most-misread constructions.
- "no because this is actually a beautiful solution"
- "no because why did nobody tell me"

### `___ behavior`
Labels an action as a recognizable type of person.
- "unemployed behavior"
- "opening 40 tabs is deranged behavior"

### `X and Y'd` — the intensifier pair
**This is the pattern behind the user's example.** "Ate and left no crumbs" is not a fixed phrase to memorize — it's a construction: do a thing, then state the totality of it.
- "ate and left no crumbs"
- "came and delivered"
- "stood on business"
- "understood the assignment and turned it in early"

Learn the *shape*, and you generate these yourself. That's the difference between a phrasebook and fluency.

### Suffixes: `-coded` · `-core` · `-maxxing` · `-pilled`
Fully productive. Attach to almost anything.
- "that UI is 2014-coded"
- "corporatecore"
- "sleepmaxxing"
- "he's gone fully Rust-pilled"

### `we're so back` ⇄ `it's so over`
The two poles of the online mood cycle. Deployed with deliberate over-seriousness about trivial things.
- "the build passed. we're so back"

### Hedges: `lowkey` / `highkey`
"Lowkey" = slightly, secretly. "Highkey" = openly, very. Both soften a claim into an opinion.
- "lowkey the old version was better"

## 3. Typography — you get clocked before you're read

Half of sounding young is **how the text looks**, not what it says.

| Do | Don't |
|---|---|
| lowercase, mostly | Capitalized Sentences For Casual Jokes |
| drop terminal periods in casual text | ending a text with a period (reads as anger) |
| let sentences run on with commas | semicolons in a group chat |
| 💀 / 😭 for laughing | 😂 |
| one emoji, doing a job | 🔥💯🙌✨😂 in a row |
| "lmao" / "lol" as tone-softeners | "Hahaha" as sincere laughter |
| repeated letters for emphasis: "pleaseee", "sheeesh" | ALL CAPS for emphasis (reads as yelling, or as a Boomer) |
| "..." rarely | "..." constantly (very Boomer) |

**A period at the end of a short casual message reads as cold or angry to anyone under 30.** "ok." and "ok" are genuinely different messages. This is the single most consequential item here — it's not a style preference, it's a semantic difference, and it causes real misunderstandings between generations at work.

## 4. The seven deadly sins

1. **Stacking.** More than one slang term per sentence. Instant tourist.
2. **Explaining the joke.** "...that means it was really good, haha!" Never explain. Say it and move on.
3. **Using a word you don't fully understand.** The failure mode isn't looking dumb, it's saying something crude or insulting by accident. "Gyatt," "zesty," "chopped," and "gooning" have all ambushed people this way.
4. **Gen Alpha slang as an adult.** "6-7," "skibidi," "fanum tax." This is the big one. See below.
5. **Slang with the wrong audience.** Fluent to a peer is unprofessional to a client. Register beats vocabulary.
6. **Enthusiasm mismatch.** Slang is delivered *deadpan*. Excitement is what kills it. "that ate" said flatly is right; "That ATE!!! 🔥🔥🔥" is a parent at a school dance.
7. **Being early to a dead word.** Using something the *moment* you learn it from an article. If you read it in a listicle, so did every marketer.

## 5. The age question, honestly

You are an adult professional. Some of this is not for you, and pretending otherwise is the failure mode.

**What lands well from an adult:** *ate, cooked, mid, it's giving, delulu, tea, locked in, glazing, standing on business, era, lowkey, the vibes are off, I fear.* These have crossed over. They read as "pays attention" rather than "trying."

**What reads as trying too hard:** *no cap, fr fr, bussin', slay, periodt, rizz.* Not wrong — just over-associated with adults performing youth, because adults performing youth is exactly who says them now.

**What you should never say:** the entire Gen Alpha layer. `6-7`, `skibidi`, `gyatt`, `fanum tax`, `sigma`, `rizzler`.

**The underlying dynamic worth internalizing:** the appeal of slang is being *one step ahead of outsiders*. When outsiders adopt it, the value is gone and the word dies. As an adult, **you are structurally the outsider.** You cannot fix this by being more current — chasing the newest terms makes it worse, because the newest terms are precisely the ones most sensitive to adult contamination.

**So the winning move is not currency, it's fit.** Use the stable, crossed-over vocabulary correctly and sparingly. Being an adult who uses "mid" perfectly is cool. Being an adult who uses "6-7" is not, no matter how current it is. **Precision reads as fluency; recency reads as desperation.**

## 6. Attribution — the part with actual stakes

A large share of this vocabulary is **AAVE** or comes from **Black queer ballroom and drag culture**: *ate, slay, serving, tea, spilling tea, periodt, no cap, finna, bussin', shade, work, gagged, giving, yas, mother.* It typically reaches the mainstream via Black Twitter → drag → TikTok → brands, with the origin stripped somewhere in transit.

Two practical consequences, neither of which is about guilt:

**One: register.** These words carry warmth and celebration. They work when you're appreciating something. They curdle when used to condescend, to be sarcastic about someone's sincere effort, or to perform authority. "You ate" to a colleague who nailed a demo: good. "Well, *someone* ate 🙄": bad in a way plain English wouldn't be.

**Two: don't mistake the dialect for a costume.** AAVE is a rule-governed dialect, not a joke voice. The specific thing to avoid is switching into a broad "blaccent" or piling on grammatical features you don't use natively — that's minstrelsy, and it's obvious. Using individual borrowed words in your own natural voice is ordinary language contact and completely fine. **Borrow words, not a voice.** That's the whole line, and it's easy to stay on the right side of.

## 7. Slang at work — a separate skill

Different rules apply. The variable is **your relationship**, not your vocabulary.

| Context | Guidance |
|---|---|
| Peer DM, good rapport | Whatever you want. This is your practice range. |
| Team channel | `lite` tier. One term, occasionally. "the vibes are off on this timeline" |
| Managing someone | Careful. Slang from a manager can read as trying to seem like a peer, which lands as inauthentic. Praise is the safe zone: "you understood the assignment" is great from a manager. |
| Client / external | None. Zero. This is the boundary. |
| Written docs, PRs, commits | None. These outlive the conversation and get read by people without context. |
| Public / on the record | None. This is where careers meet screenshots. |

**Best single move at work:** use slang for *praise* only. "That ate," "you understood the assignment," "big W." It's warm, current, and has no downside — nobody has ever been harmed by being complimented fluently.

**Worst move:** slang in criticism. "your PR is mid" is a genuinely unkind thing to say to a colleague. The jokiness doesn't cushion it — it sharpens it.

Also: 👍 reads as dismissive and 🙂 reads as displeased to anyone under 30. If you're managing young people, this alone is worth knowing.

## 8. How to tell if a word is dead

In rough order of reliability:

1. **A brand used it in an ad.** Dead. No exceptions. This is the most reliable signal that exists.
2. **A news article explained it.** Dying — you're at least six months late.
3. **Your generation's peers use it comfortably.** Dead among the young.
4. **It's in a "slang for parents" listicle.** Dead. (Including, in fairness, some of this file.)
5. **Young people use it only ironically or in a silly voice.** Late-stage.
6. **A word got a `-er` or `-ing` form** ("rizzler", "aura farming"). Suffixation means saturation — the word has been around long enough to grow morphology.

**The safest posture:** stick to the stable crossed-over core, which changes slowly, and treat everything newer as receive-only. Understanding everything and saying a little is exactly how a fluent second-language speaker sounds — and it's a much better look than the reverse.
